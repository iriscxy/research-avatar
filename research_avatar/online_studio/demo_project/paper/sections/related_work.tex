\section{Related Work}

\label{sec:related-work}

% PAPER_STUDIO_PARAGRAPH:R-P1

The closest methodological thread concerns how large language models respond to superficial variations in their input. Prior work has examined interventions on input structure, including the ordering of answer options in multiple-choice tasks \cite{}, and some studies report that models can produce different outputs when the same options are presented in a different sequence \cite{}. This thread primarily documents the phenomenon and proposes remedies such as prompt engineering and fine-tuning strategies \cite{}. However, it does not systematically characterize when the effect is large, when it vanishes, or which aspects of the model's decision process it might reflect. The boundary of this thread is therefore descriptive rather than mechanistic, leaving open the question of whether option-order sensitivity is a stable property of the model's reasoning or a byproduct of its decoding procedure. This open question motivates the controlled study proposed in this paper, which will vary only the option permutation while holding all other inputs fixed.

% PAPER_STUDIO_PARAGRAPH:R-P2

A separate thread addresses the evaluation of multiple-choice question answering \cite{}, where the standard protocol samples either a single option ordering or a small fixed set of orderings for each item. These benchmarks \cite{} typically treat option position as a nuisance variable, averaging accuracy across repeated presentations or reporting a single pass without controlling for permutation. As a result, the evaluation literature does not provide a direct measure of how much a model's answer depends on the specific sequence in which options appear, nor does it establish whether observed score differences across orderings exceed the noise of the evaluation procedure itself. The distinction for this paper is that it will treat option ordering as the independent variable rather than a controlled nuisance, measuring the full distribution of outcomes across all permutations of the same item while holding every other input component fixed.

% PAPER_STUDIO_PARAGRAPH:R-P3

Together, these two threads define the space this paper occupies. The methodological thread \cite{} establishes that option-order sensitivity is a real and observable phenomenon but stops short of explaining when or why it occurs. The evaluation thread \cite{}, by contrast, assumes the effect is small enough to ignore and therefore does not measure it directly. This paper sits at the intersection: it takes the phenomenon documented by the first thread as its object of study and applies the controlled measurement discipline advocated by the second. Specifically, it will isolate option permutation as the sole variable, examine the full distribution of outcomes across all orderings of a fixed item, and report the resulting sensitivity without relying on aggregate scores that could conceal it. This position is neither a new mitigation technique nor a new benchmark, but a systematic characterization of a previously under-measured property of multiple-choice answer generation.
