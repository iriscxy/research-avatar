1. Problem

You should read this field as a coordination problem, not simply a dialogue-generation problem. A commerce response may be fluent yet wrong about a user’s eligibility, faithful yet unhelpful, persuasive yet unsafe, or accurate yet too slow for production. The core object is therefore a vector of outcomes whose components are measured by different instruments and whose acceptable trade-offs depend on the dialogue state.
User profile + dialogue history→Grounded reasoning↔Natural response→Task / business outcome↔Latency + cost

Five search angles organize the evidence: grounded TOD architectures, preference and policy learning, adaptive dialogue-specific reward coordination, general multi-objective optimization, and evaluation/deployment. The key assumptions that separate papers are whether objectives are commensurable, whether conflicts are observable from minibatch gradients, whether a single policy should serve all preferences, and whether offline judges predict online outcomes.

Scope boundary. Multi-task learning papers are relevant only as mechanisms for coordinating objectives; they do not by themselves validate dialogue rewards. Conversely, strong TOD generation results do not establish a good multi-objective optimizer unless per-objective behavior and trade-offs are exposed.

2. Approaches

The useful taxonomy is the decision the designer makes: where to represent grounding, how to acquire preference signals, where to combine objectives, and whether to return one compromise or a controllable set of policies.

Grounded TOD architectures

Inclusion rule. Include work whose main decision is how user state, database evidence, policy, and response generation are represented. Within-family comparison. Unified sequence models reduce interfaces; modular adapters and neuro-symbolic components retain audit points; prompting and agents trade training cost for inference dependence.

Live debate — static scalarization versus specialized optimizers. Pareto and gradient-conflict work argues that a fixed weighted sum can miss balanced solutions, but generalization analyses show that volatile conflict-avoidant weights may themselves hurt population risk. Dialogue papers rarely run both diagnostics under matched compute, so “adaptive” is not yet synonymous with “better trade-off.”
Live debate — one adaptive policy versus objective specialists. MORE and dynamic-reward dialogue methods seek one deployable policy; EMORL retains specialist models and combines them. The former favors serving efficiency, the latter preserves separable expertise and post-training control. Existing comparisons do not hold memory, latency, and preference coverage constant.

3. Evaluation

Evaluation regimes answer different questions. You cannot collapse benchmark success, judge scores, human preferences, and conversion into one “quality” number without changing the claim.

A. Static public benchmarks

Dataset realism
MultiWOZ and related schemas expose state, actions, and reference responses but underrepresent personalized arithmetic and commercial policy.
Shift source
Domain, schema, annotation noise, unseen slot combinations.
Metric contract
Belief-state accuracy, Inform, Success, Combined, lexical diversity; report uncertainty across seeds/checkpoints.
Efficiency
Training budget and inference tokens are often omitted, limiting fair comparison with reasoning-heavy systems.

B. Interactive simulation

Dataset realism
User simulators permit complete dialogues and policy errors, but simulator agreement is not user validity.
Shift source
Persona, goal complexity, adversarial or recovery turns, simulator family.
Metric contract
Goal completion plus turn-level policy, knowledge, cohesion, and recovery errors.
Efficiency
Count model calls, tokens, retries, and tool/API actions per successful dialogue.

C. Human and LLM judgments

Dataset realism
Pairwise and rubric judgments can target naturalness, faithfulness, helpfulness, and preference.
Shift source
Judge model, prompt, order, verbosity, social framing, evaluator population.
Metric contract
Agreement, rank correlation, tie calibration, confidence intervals, and blinded human validation.
Efficiency
Report annotation hours/cost and judge-token cost; do not treat one judge as ground truth.

D. Production experiments

Dataset realism
Live traffic measures actual user behavior and business constraints, but is private and policy-dependent.
Shift source
Traffic mix, campaign, season, eligibility rules, agent handoff, and model rollout.
Metric contract
Randomized lift with exposure definition, uncertainty, guardrails, satisfaction, handoff, and error severity.
Efficiency
End-to-end latency, output length, serving cost, escalation cost, and failure recovery.

Representative evaluation evidence

Inclusion rule. These papers primarily define an evaluation object, dataset, simulator, judge, or measurement warning. Comparability limit. Static references test response resemblance; interactive regimes test policy trajectories; human/LLM judgments test perceived qualities; production A/B tests measure context-specific outcomes.

4. Gaps

Recent trends

  • From response generation to interactive agency. Recent TOD work adds API use, proactive planning, user simulation, and turn-level policy checks; the unit of evaluation is becoming a trajectory rather than a sentence.
  • From one reward to structured feedback. Dialogue-specific work increasingly separates consistency, reasoning, fluency, and naturalness, while general MOO supplies conflict, fairness, and Pareto tools.
  • From human-only feedback to scalable judges. RLAIF and LLM-based evaluation reduce labeling cost, while judge-audit papers expose framing, order, verbosity, and tie-calibration risks.
  • From offline quality to deployment contracts. Production-oriented claims increasingly need latency, serving cost, handoff, satisfaction, and randomized business outcomes alongside benchmark metrics.
Structural gap 1 — conflict is claimed but rarely identified. Dialogue papers report aggregate reward curves, while optimization papers define conflict through gradient geometry. The missing evidence is a time-resolved link from reward disagreement → gradient interaction → per-objective behavior under matched stochasticity.
Structural gap 2 — reward validity and optimization quality are confounded. A better optimizer can look worse when a judge is biased, and a better judge can make a static optimizer look sufficient. A factorial design crossing reward source, aggregation rule, and independent human validation is largely absent.
Structural gap 3 — preference coverage is not deployment coverage. Pareto-set and directional methods expose diverse trade-offs, while one-policy systems optimize an implicit operating point. No common protocol measures how well either strategy covers heterogeneous users under one latency and memory budget.
Structural gap 4 — public reproducibility and commercial realism remain split. MultiWOZ enables controlled comparison; production logs expose profile arithmetic, policy constraints, and real outcomes. A privacy-preserving public benchmark pairing executable profiles, interactive policies, calibrated judges, and cost accounting would connect the two regimes.
Research opening. The deepest short-paper experiment is not another aggregate leaderboard. It is a controlled diagnostic that logs per-objective rewards and gradients, compares static weighting with one adaptive rule under identical samples, validates the reward dimensions against blinded human judgments, and reports the Pareto change with latency/token cost.
